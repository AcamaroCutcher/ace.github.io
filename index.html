Research projects

Wildfire surrogates, molecular dynamics, monsoon forecasting, pure mathematics and plasmonics.

  1. Current2025 – present · Portland State University, Mathematics & Statistics

    Modified Fourier Neural Operators for Direct Wildfire Spread Prediction: A FourCastNet Adaptation

    Re-engineers FourCastNet, a global weather emulator, into a direct solver for wildfire spread. Fuel moisture, LANDFIRE vegetation density and high-resolution topography are added to the model's input channels, and its Adaptive Fourier Neural Operator is retrained on historical fire perimeters so that it learns how the fire front evolves. FourCastNet produces global weather ensembles about 80,000 times faster than numerical models (Kurth et al., 2022); the goal is a surrogate that brings that speed to fire-spread ensembles, which coupled physics models such as WRF-SFIRE compute too slowly for operational use.

    PythonDeep learningNeural operatorsSurrogate modelsHPC
    PSU wildfire capstone code Related: Forest Park Wildfires

  2. Summer 2026 · Los Alamos National Laboratory

    Melting of Tin from Molecular Dynamics with a Hybrid Machine-Learned Potential

    Molecular dynamics simulations of tin in LAMMPS, driven by a hybrid machine-learned interatomic potential. First-passage-time dynamics, the statistics of how long the system takes to first cross from one state to another, are used to understand how tin melts.

    LAMMPSMolecular dynamicsMachine-learned potentialsFirst-passage times

  3. Current2024 – present · McNair Scholars / CCAR REU, Portland State University

    Hybrid Data-Driven and Dynamical Forecasting of the South Asian Monsoon Intraseasonal Oscillation

    Dynamical models struggle to predict the active and break phases of the South Asian monsoon, which are driven by northward-propagating rainfall anomalies known as MISOs. This project works with Ensemble Oscillation Correction (EnOC), a method introduced by Bach et al. (2021) and applied to the monsoon in Bach et al. (2024). It uses the near-regularity of the oscillation to correct physics-based ensemble forecasts with data-driven ones, improving rainfall prediction three to four weeks ahead, a range that matters for agricultural and hydrological planning.

    Python / JuliaData-driven forecastingDynamical modelsClimate
    Code (my fork of EnOC.jl)

  4. Completed2023 – 2024 · Math honors thesis, LSAMP research scholar, Portland State University

    Minimal Group Embeddings

    Classifies small finite groups and finds their minimal embedding degree: the smallest n for which G embeds in the symmetric group Sn. Cayley's theorem guarantees an embedding into S|G|; using the Sylow theorems to pin down each group's structure, the minimal degree often turns out to be far smaller than the group's order.

    Abstract algebraGroup theorySylow theorems
    Thesis (PDF) Google Drive

  5. CompletedWestern Washington University, Physics & Astronomy

    Multiscale Theoretical Modeling of Surface Plasmon Polaritons: From Fundamentals to Integrated Photonic Applications

    Derives surface plasmon polariton dispersion relations by applying Bloch's theorem to Maxwell's equations, treating a metal–insulator multilayer as a one-dimensional plasmonic crystal. The model shows how periodic modulation creates band structures and forbidden gaps, a guide for tailoring the optical response of superlattice metal–insulator–metal devices.

    Maxwell's equationsBloch's theoremSolid-state physicsMathematica
    Code

  6. PublishedPLOS ONE, 2022 · Western Washington University

    Excitation of “Forbidden” Guided-Wave Plasmon Polariton Modes via Direct Reflectance Using a Low Refractive Index Polymer Coupling Layer

    An experimental study of plasmonic modes in metal–insulator–metal thin films. With a low-index polymer coupling layer and angle-resolved reflectance spectroscopy, we excited and characterized guided-wave plasmon polariton modes that conventional methods cannot reach, and showed how the polymer layer changes the coupling conditions. Co-authored with nine colleagues at Western Washington University.

About

A little about me

I'm a first-year PhD student in mathematics at Portland State University, working on wildfire modeling with machine learning.

My research focuses on learning fast surrogate models of wildfire: models that reproduce what expensive physics simulations predict, quickly enough to be used operationally. It combines physics, mathematics and computation, and I like learning from the people I work on it with.

When I'm not doing research you can find me tinkering with electronics or code, traveling, attending conferences, and helping respond to humanitarian emergencies.
