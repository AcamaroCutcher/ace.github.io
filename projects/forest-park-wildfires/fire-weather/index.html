Fire weather

A fuel map and terrain say where fire can go; the weather decides where it does. To choose realistic wind and dryness for the simulations, and real dates to run WRF-SFIRE on, I analysed hourly observations from June to October, 2000–2025, at the four airports around the park: Portland International (13 km east of the park's centre), Hillsboro (15 km west), Scappoose (23 km north) and Troutdale (28 km east, at the mouth of the Columbia River Gorge).

Background: Forest Park terrain from USGS 3DEP, tinted with this project's data

Figure 11

Dangerous winds come from the east

I counted an hour as dry and windy when relative humidity was 25% or lower and the wind reached 15 mph, or gusts 25 mph. That's my screening threshold, not the National Weather Service's official Red Flag criteria.

On an ordinary summer hour the wind at Portland International blows from the northwest. On dry and windy hours, 92% blow from the northeast to southeast. This is the east wind that pours out of the Columbia River Gorge when high pressure builds east of the Cascades, bringing dry inland air. Hillsboro, on the far side of the park, sees the same thing (83%). Scappoose is the exception, but it logged only 46 such hours in 26 years.

Figure 12

Late summer, not midsummer

Portland International averages about 17 dry and windy hours a year, and 76% fall in September or October. July is hot but rarely windy enough. The danger window is the end of the dry season, when fuels have had all summer to dry and the first east-wind events of autumn arrive before the rains.

Figure 13

The case study: Labor Day 2020

The same east wind drove the 2020 Labor Day fires in the Oregon Cascades. At Portland International it lasted from the afternoon of 7 September to the evening of the 9th: 44 dry and windy hours, sustained winds to 36 mph, gusts to 50 mph and humidity down to 8%.

The bottom panel estimates the moisture of fine dead fuel (twigs, grass, needles) from temperature and humidity. It fell from 16% to about 4% within a day and reached 2% on the 9th. Fuels that dry burn readily.

Figure 14

Where wind and slope work together

Fire spreads fastest when wind and slope push the same way. Coloured cells are slopes of 10° or more that face into the wind, so the wind drives fire straight uphill there.

An east wind lines up with 42% of the park's slopes: the river-facing hillside, from Linnton and Highway 30 up to the ridge. Over the ridge, a fire would be heading for the homes along Skyline Boulevard and the Washington County side. The usual northwest wind lines up with 28% and pushes fire along the park's length, toward its southeast end in NW Portland.

Limitations

  • Airports, not the park. The stations are 13–28 km away on open, flat ground. Inside the park, terrain channels the wind and the forest floor stays moister than air-based estimates suggest. WRF-SFIRE will resolve the local wind; the fuel moisture still needs care.
  • Dead fuel only. The moisture estimate covers fine dead fuel from that hour's temperature and humidity. It ignores rain in the days before (common before October events) and the moisture of living plants.
  • Hours, not fires. A dry, windy hour is an opportunity, not a prediction. Ignition is a separate question.
