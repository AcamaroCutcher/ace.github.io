The math of FIRETEC

FIRETEC, from Los Alamos National Laboratory, computes fire behavior from the physics of burning vegetation and moving air instead of a spread-rate formula. Its equations, how they are solved, and where to get the code.

A different kind of fire model

WRF-SFIRE (Sections 06–14) moves the fire front with a formula: Rothermel's spread rate tells the level set how fast to advance, and the atmosphere only feels the heat the burning fuel releases. FIRETEC, developed at Los Alamos National Laboratory by Rodman Linn and colleagues (Linn 1997; Linn et al. 2002), has no spread-rate formula at all. It solves conservation equations for the gas and for the vegetation in every cell of a fine 3-D grid, and the fire's spread, shape and intensity come out of how heat moves from burning fuel to unburned fuel through the air: by convection, radiation and turbulent mixing. Linn et al. call this a self-determining model.

The two are complementary. FIRETEC can tell us why a fire behaves as it does in a particular stand of trees; WRF-SFIRE can run the whole park for days. LANL's faster tool QUIC-Fire (Linn et al. 2020) sits in between: it couples a fast wind solver with a simplified fire model to capture FIRETEC-like fire–wind feedbacks quickly enough for prescribed-fire planning on a desktop computer.

Where the equations on this page come from Equations (1)–(9) are transcribed from Linn, Reisner, Colman and Winterkamp (2002), the standard published description. Where the 2002 paper leaves a coefficient unspecified ("described in Linn 1997"), the formula shown is the one in the open FIRETEC-1.5.1 source on GitHub, with a link to the file, so every number can be checked.

Resolved volumes and averaged equations

A 2 m FIRETEC cell holds thousands of grass blades or a piece of a tree crown. Their positions are never known, so FIRETEC works with averages over a resolved volume: the bulk density of fuel (kg of dry fuel per m³ of space), its moisture, its surface-area-to-volume ratio, and the mean temperatures of the solid and the gas. Each instantaneous variable is split into a mean and a fluctuation, \(\phi = \overline{\phi} + \phi'\), and the conservation laws are ensemble-averaged. Averaging a product leaves correlations such as \(\overline{u_i' u_j'}\) (turbulent stresses) or \(\overline{\rho_{\text{wood}}'\rho_{O_2}'}\) (how often fuel and oxygen are actually in the same place), and these need models: that is where most of FIRETEC's physics lives.

Flames are not resolved. Instead, FIRETEC estimates what fraction of a resolved volume is hot enough to burn from a probability distribution of temperature inside the cell (below), and lets the reaction rate depend on that fraction, on the oxygen supply and on turbulent mixing.

One reaction for all of combustion

The hundreds of reactions in burning vegetation are lumped into a single solid–gas reaction that turns wood and oxygen into inert products and heat:

$$N_{\text{wood}}\,(\text{wood}) + N_{O_2}\,(\text{oxygen}) \;\longrightarrow\; (\text{products}) + (\text{heat}) \tag{1}$$

Eq. (1)The stoichiometric coefficients give the mass of wood and of oxygen consumed per unit mass of products, through pyrolysis and all the intermediate reactions. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (1).

ConstantValue in FIRETEC-1.5.1Meaning
\(N_{\text{wood}}\)0.4552kg of dry fuel per kg of products (Drysdale)
\(N_{O_2}\)0.5448kg of oxygen per kg of products; \(N_{\text{wood}} + N_{O_2} = 1\)
\(H_{\text{wood}}\)8.913 MJ kg⁻¹heat of reaction per kg of products (the 2002 paper uses 8914 kJ kg⁻¹)
\(T_{\text{pyr}}\)600 Ktcrit: temperature at which half of a cell's fuel is taken to be reacting
\(H_{\text{water}}\)2.257 MJ kg⁻¹latent heat of evaporation of fuel moisture at 373 K
\(c_{p,\text{wood}}\)2500 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹heat capacity of the fuel (cpwood, a gridlist input)
\(\rho_{\text{micro}}\)500 kg m⁻³density of the fuel material itself (rhoMicro, a gridlist input)

Constants: Vegetation/variables_veg.F90.

The reaction rate: mixing-limited burning

The rate at which fuel is consumed (kg m⁻³ s⁻¹ of products) is

$$F_{\text{wood}} = \rho_{\text{wood}}\,\rho_{O_2}\,\sigma\,\Pi \tag{2}$$

Eq. (2)Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (2). σ is a turbulent mixing coefficient and Π gathers the temperature distribution and the proportions of the reactants.

The philosophy, in the paper's words, is that pyrolysis is driven by the heat flux to the solid, which comes from gas-phase reactions limited by how fast oxygen is mixed in. If either fuel or oxygen is missing, the chain breaks. In the 1.5.1 source (Vegetation/burn_veg.F90) the factors are written out as

$$F_{\text{wood}} = \frac{c_F\,\rho_{\text{wood}}\,\rho_{O_2}\,\sigma_c\,\psi_s\,\lambda_{of}}{100\,s^{2}},\qquad \sigma_c = \tfrac12\,s_c\sqrt{0.2\,k_B},\qquad \lambda_{of} = \frac{\rho_{\text{wood}}\,\rho_{O_2}}{\left(\rho_{\text{wood}}/N_{\text{wood}} + \rho_{O_2}/N_{O_2}\right)^{2}} \tag{3}$$

Eq. (3)The rate as coded in burnFuel. The constant 100 and the factor 0.2 are in the source as written.

\(\psi_s\)
fraction of the solid in the cell hotter than the reaction threshold: a cumulative distribution of solid temperature, Eq. (5)
\(\lambda_{of}\)
largest when fuel and oxygen are in stoichiometric proportion, \(\rho_{\text{wood}}/\rho_{O_2} = N_{\text{wood}}/N_{O_2}\)
\(\sigma_c\)
turbulent mixing speed at the smallest (flame) scale; \(k_B\) is the turbulent kinetic energy at the fuel scale, \(s_c = 0.1\) m
\(s\)
fuel size scale (m): thin fuels have more surface per unit mass and burn faster, as \(1/s^2\)
\(c_F\)
0.9 while volatile fuel remains, falling linearly to 0.09 (char burning) once less than 40% of the initial fuel is left, Eq. (4)
$$c_F = 0.9\,p + 0.09\,(1-p),\qquad p = \max\!\left(0,\;\frac{\rho_{\text{wood}} - 0.4\,\rho_{\text{wood},0}}{0.6\,\rho_{\text{wood},0}}\right) \tag{4}$$

Eq. (4)Flaming to smoldering: p is the fraction of the volatile ("hydrocarbon") part of the fuel that is left.

The temperature distribution \(\psi_s\)

A cell with a mean solid temperature of 500 K may contain some fuel at 700 K next to a flame and some still at 350 K. FIRETEC represents this with \(\psi_s(\bar T_s)\), the expected fraction of the solid above the reaction threshold. Three shapes are available through the ifuel input; all equal ½ at \(T_{\text{pyr}} = 600\) K. The default (ifuel = 1) is a piecewise curve made of a straight ramp joined to two circular arcs between 310 K and 890 K; option 3 is

$$\psi_s(T_s) = \tfrac12\left[1 + \operatorname{erf}\!\big(0.0079\,(T_s - 600\,\text{K})\big)\right]\quad\text{for }310 < T_s < 890\text{ K}, \tag{5}$$

Eq. (5)ifuel = 3 in computePsif: 0 below 310 K and 1 above 890 K. Option 2 evaluates the same erf curve with a polynomial approximation and no cut-offs.

Where the heat goes

Not all of the heat \(H_{\text{wood}}F_{\text{wood}}\) stays in the cell's gas. A fraction \(\Theta\) goes straight back into the solid. It is small while the fuel is flaming (most of the heat leaves with the gases, and less when oxygen is scarce relative to fuel) and grows to \(\theta_g = 0.75\) as the fuel turns to char and glows:

$$\Theta = p\,(1-\theta_g)\exp\!\left(-\frac{N_{O_2}}{N_{\text{wood}}}\,\psi_s\,\frac{\rho_{\text{wood}}}{\rho_{O_2}}\right) + (1-p)\,\theta_g,\qquad \theta_g = 0.75 \tag{6}$$

Eq. (6)The share of the reaction heat deposited in the solid (thetaSolid in burnFuel). The 2002 paper describes it as "a linearly increasing function of the amount of wood that has burned".

The solid phase: fuel, water and temperature

Each fuel type in each cell carries three variables: dry fuel density, water density and temperature. Fuel and water are only consumed; they do not move.

$$\frac{\partial \rho_{\text{wood}}}{\partial t} = -N_{\text{wood}}\,F_{\text{wood}} \tag{7}$$

Eq. (7)Fuel consumed by the reaction. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (3).

$$\frac{\partial \rho_{\text{water}}}{\partial t} = -F_{\text{water}} \tag{8}$$

Eq. (8)Moisture lost by evaporation. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (4).

$$\begin{aligned}\left(c_{p,\text{water}}\rho_{\text{water}} + c_{p,\text{wood}}\rho_{\text{wood}}\right)\frac{\partial T_s}{\partial t} = {}& Q_{\text{rad}} + h\,a_v\,(T_{\text{gas}} - T_s) - F_{\text{water}}\left(H_{\text{water}} + c_{p,\text{water}}T_{\text{boil}}\right) \\ &+ F_{\text{wood}}\left(\Theta H_{\text{wood}} - c_{p,\text{wood}}\,T_{\text{pyr}}N_{\text{wood}}\right)\end{aligned} \tag{9}$$

Eq. (9)Energy of the solid. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (5): net thermal radiation, convective exchange with the gas, the latent and sensible heat carried off by evaporating water, and the reaction heat kept by the solid minus the sensible heat carried off by the pyrolysis gases.

Evaporation

Like burning, drying is spread over a range of mean temperatures because the cell's temperature varies inside. In Vegetation/evaporation_veg.F90 the evaporated fraction of the initial water follows a ramp \(\psi_w(T_s)\) that never decreases:

$$\psi_w = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\frac{T_s - T_a}{2T_{\text{boil}} - 2T_a},\,0,\,1\right),\qquad T_a = T_\infty + (T_{\text{boil}} - T_\infty)\,\frac{310 - T_\infty}{T_{\text{pyr}} - T_\infty} \tag{10}$$

Eq. (10)For an ambient \(T_\infty\) = 300 K the water goes between 302 K and 444 K, centred on \(T_{\text{boil}}\) = 373 K. \(F_{\text{water}}\) is the rate that keeps \(\rho_{\text{water}} = \rho_{\text{water},0}(1-\psi_w)\).

Convective heating

The exchange coefficient comes from a correlation for flow across a cylinder (Incropera and DeWitt), with an effective speed that includes turbulence (Vegetation/convection_veg.F90):

$$\begin{gathered}h = \frac{0.683}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Re}^{0.466}\,\frac{k_{\text{air}}}{s},\qquad \mathrm{Re} = \frac{s\,U_{\text{eff}}}{\nu},\\ U_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{k_A + 1.2\,k_B} + |\mathbf u_{\text{fuel}}|,\qquad a_v = \frac{2\,\rho_{\text{wood}}}{\rho_{\text{micro}}\,s}\end{gathered} \tag{11}$$

Eq. (11)\(k_{\text{air}}\) = 0.0338 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, \(\nu\) = 1.5×10⁻⁵ m² s⁻¹. \(a_v\) is the fuel surface area per unit volume of space. The 1/π is an average over the orientation of the fuel elements.

The lab below integrates Eq. (9) for one resolved volume of grass bathed in gas at a fixed temperature (as at the edge of a flame), with the same ramps, correlation and reaction rate as the code. Radiation is simplified to an exchange between the fuel and surroundings at the gas temperature (emissivity 0.9) instead of FIRETEC's Monte Carlo transport, and the gas does not respond, so it shows the solid's side of the story: the moisture delays heating, the fuel reacts only slowly until its mean temperature nears 600 K, and whether it burns at all depends on the gas temperature and the oxygen supply.

The gas phase

The air is a compressible gas whose averaged density, momentum, potential temperature and oxygen are transported on the grid. Oxygen is advected, diffused by turbulence and consumed by the reaction:

$$\frac{\partial \rho_{O_2}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (u_i\,\rho_{O_2})}{\partial x_i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\!\left(\sigma\,\frac{\partial (\rho_{O_2}/\rho_{\text{gas}})}{\partial x_j}\right) - F_{\text{wood}}\,N_{O_2} \tag{12}$$

Eq. (12)Oxygen. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (6).

$$\frac{\partial \rho_{\text{gas}}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (u_i\,\rho_{\text{gas}})}{\partial x_i} = F_{\text{wood}}\,N_{\text{wood}} + F_{\text{water}} \tag{13}$$

Eq. (13)Total gas mass: the burned fuel and the evaporated water become gas. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (7).

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial (\rho_{\text{gas}} u_i)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (u_i u_j\,\rho_{\text{gas}})}{\partial x_j} = {}& -\frac{\partial R_{ij}}{\partial x_j} - \frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i} + \rho_{\text{gas}}\,g_i \\ &- \frac{3\,C_D\,\rho_{\text{wood}}\,\rho_{\text{gas}}\,|\mathbf u|\,u_i}{8\,s\,\rho_{\text{micro}}}\end{aligned} \tag{14}$$

Eq. (14)Momentum. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (8): advection, turbulent (Reynolds) stresses, pressure gradient, buoyancy and drag by the vegetation, with \(C_D \approx 1\).

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial (\rho_{\text{gas}}\theta)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (\theta u_j\,\rho_{\text{gas}})}{\partial x_j} = {}& \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\!\left(\sigma\,\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial x_j}\right) \\ &+ \frac{\theta}{c_p}\left[\frac{h\,a_v\,(T_s - T_{\text{gas}}) + Q_{\text{rad,gas}} + F_{\text{wood}}\,(1-\Theta)\,H_{\text{wood}}}{T_{\text{gas}}}\right]\end{aligned} \tag{15}$$

Eq. (15)Potential temperature of the gas. Linn et al. (2002), Eq. (9): convective exchange with the solid (the mirror of Eq. 9 above), radiation absorbed by the gas, and the share 1 − Θ of the reaction heat.

The drag term is what makes a canopy slow the wind and generate turbulence. In the 1.5.1 code (Vegetation/drag_veg.F90) it is written as \(\tfrac12 C_D\,a'_v\,|\mathbf u|\,\rho_{\text{gas}}u_i\) with \(a'_v = 2\rho_{\text{wood}}/(\pi s\,\rho_{\text{micro}})\), a coefficient of \(1/\pi \approx 0.32\) instead of the paper's 3/8, and the bottom cell includes a correlation for grass bending in the wind. The paper is explicit that both the drag term and \(C_D\) were subjects of ongoing research.

Turbulence at three scales

FIRETEC's cells are larger than the eddies shed by branches and much larger than the mixing zones around flames, so turbulence is modelled. Linn (1997) tracks the turbulent kinetic energy at three length scales, each with its own transport equation:

ScaleSizeCreated byUsed for
Athe cell or the canopy (largest unresolved eddies)wind shearturbulent stresses \(R_{ij}\) and diffusion of heat and species
Bthe spacing of fuel elementswind flowing through vegetation (wakes)mixing of oxygen into the fuel bed, \(\sigma_c\) in Eq. (3)
Cflame and fuel-element sizetransfer from the larger scalesthe mixing that limits combustion

Each equation includes production by velocity shear and by wind–canopy interaction, transfer of energy between the scales, diffusion and dissipation. The stresses use a Boussinesq form, schematically

$$R_{ij} = \tfrac23\,\rho\,k\,\delta_{ij} - \rho\,K\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right),\qquad K \sim \ell\,\sqrt{k}, \tag{16}$$

Eq. (16)Generic Boussinesq closure: an eddy diffusivity K from a length scale and a turbulent kinetic energy. FIRETEC's version is anisotropic and built from the three prescribed length scales (Linn 1997); see subGridTurb/ for the coded form.

In the code the scale-A and scale-B energies are transported variables (ka, kb) next to u, v, w and θ, switched on with iturb. Later work compared this closure with standard LES sub-filter models over canopies.

Radiation

Thermal radiation preheats fuel ahead of the fire. The 2002 paper used a two-field, diffusion-like transport of radiant energy (adapted from Stephens 1984): energy emitted by hot gas, according to a temperature distribution in the cell, spreads through the medium and is absorbed by fuel according to its projected surface area per unit volume. It is cheaper but less accurate than discrete-ordinates methods.

FIRETEC-1.5.1 replaces this with a Monte Carlo method (Radiation/monteCarlo_rad.F90): photon packets are shot from emitting cells and absorbed by the fuel and gas they cross, giving the radiative source terms \(Q_{\text{rad}}\) in Eqs. (9) and (15). It also adds solar heating of fuels from the sun's position (Radiation/solarHeat_rad.F90).

HIGRAD: how the equations are solved

The gas equations are solved by HIGRAD (Reisner et al. 2000), a finite-volume, fully compressible atmospheric code.

  • Terrain-following grid. The vertical coordinate follows the ground and stretches with height, so cells are under a metre thick at the surface and tens of metres aloft. With ground height \(z_s(x,y)\) and domain top \(z_b\), the Jacobian is \(G = z_b / \big[(z_b - z_s)\,g'(\zeta)\big]\), where \(g\) is a cubic stretching set by aa1 (metric.F90).
  • Advection with MPDATA (Smolarkiewicz and Margolin 1998): an upwind donor-cell step followed by antidiffusive corrections that keep it second order and free of new extrema, so densities and temperatures never go negative or overshoot. The donor-cell flux at a face is
$$\Phi_{i+\frac12} = \max(c_{i+\frac12},0)\,\phi_i + \min(c_{i+\frac12},0)\,\phi_{i+1},\qquad \phi_i^{\,n+1} = \phi_i^{\,n} - G_i^{-1}\left(\Phi_{i+\frac12} - \Phi_{i-\frac12}\right) \tag{17}$$

Eq. (17)Donor-cell (upwind) update in one direction, with Courant number c = uΔt/Δx at each face. The Fortran is in donorcell (higrad.F90) and donor (advectionScheme.F90); the Fortran course builds the same scheme step by step.

  • Method of averages (MOA). Sound waves are thousands of times faster than the wind, so the pressure and buoyancy terms are stepped on nts small time steps inside each large step dt, using the averaged velocities for advection on the large step (Reisner et al. 2000; MOA.F90).
  • Fuel on the same grid. The fuel arrays have an extra index for fuel type, xvfuel(ift, i, j, k, var), and are read from files made by LANL's Trees program (bulk density, size scale, moisture and depth for each type).
  • Ignition raises the solid temperature in prescribed cells, as in the 2002 runs (fuel in a 4 m × 20 m box heated from 300 to 500 K over 1 s).

The main loop in compress.F90 is short and shows the order of operations:

compress.F90 (FIRETEC-1.5.1, abridged; BSD-3-Clause, © 2026 Triad National Security / LANL)
call input              ! read the 'gridlist' input file
call setup_MPI          ! split the domain over nprocx x nprocy processes
call definearray        ! allocate the fields
call rinit              ! initial wind, temperature, fuels
do while (ittot < nt)
  call explicit_domain_updates   ! pressure, gas temperature, halo exchange
  call small_explicit_forcings   ! forcings applied on every small step
  call MOA                       ! method of averages: nts small steps
  call large_explicit_forcings   ! fuel physics, turbulence, radiation
  call forcing                   ! apply all forcings, advance one large step
  time = time + dt
  if (mod(ittot, frqoutput) == 0) call binary_writeio(ittot)
  it = it + 1
end do
call mpi_finalize(ierror)

Running on many processors

FIRETEC is written in Fortran 90 and parallelised with MPI. The horizontal grid of n × m columns is cut into nprocx × nprocy rectangular blocks, one per MPI process, each with ih layers of ghost (halo) cells around it. Before any step that needs a neighbour's values, the routine update exchanges the edge rows with MPI_Sendrecv; at the domain edges it applies the boundary condition instead (cyclic, relaxation to the ambient wind, or mirror). The grid must divide evenly: n divisible by nprocx and m by nprocy, and the run must be started with exactly nprocx × nprocy processes (msga.F90).

The defaults are a 200 × 200 × 40 grid at 2 m on 8 × 8 = 64 processes. A 1 km × 1 km piece of Forest Park at 2 m with 60 levels is 15 million cells, a typical job for a few hundred cores. Fortran for FIRETEC, a separate project on this site, teaches the Fortran and MPI needed to read and change this code.

Is the code available?

Yes. In June 2026 LANL published HIGRAD/FIRETEC on GitHub at github.com/lanl/FIRETEC. The default branch is FIRETEC-1.5.1, a checkpoint release; develop has the newest work, and the branches FIRETEC-1.1.0 to 1.5.0 keep older versions. The repository's LICENSE file is the BSD 3-Clause license, and the README cites LANL open-source release O5086. It contains about 12,000 lines of Fortran 90 in CODE-1.5.1/ (vegetation, radiation, turbulence, firebrands, emissions and sensor modules), example inputs and fuel maps in TOOLS/, and a growing DOCS/. LANL's technology-licensing page lists a FIRETEC 2.x for license agreements, so the public code may lag the internal version.

Building and running

FIRETEC needs a Fortran compiler (gfortran, Intel or Cray), an MPI library and CMake. The build follows the repository's CMakeLists.txt; the executable is called firerad.

Build (Linux or macOS, with gfortran, Open MPI and CMake installed)
git clone https://github.com/lanl/FIRETEC.git
cd FIRETEC/CODE-1.5.1
mkdir build && cd build
cmake ..                 # add -DDBL_PREC=ON for double precision, -DDEBUG=ON for bounds checking
make -j 8                # produces ./firerad

A run reads a text file called gridlist from the working directory, one name = value per line (all options are listed in TOOLS/Gridlist/GRIDLIST_OPTIONS.txt). The example gridlist runs a 50 × 50 × 20 grid on 2 × 2 processes:

gridlist (from TOOLS/Gridlist, abridged)
irst      = 0          ! 0 = fresh start, 1 = restart
nt        = 1000       ! number of large time steps
frqoutput = 1000       ! output every 1000 steps
nprocx    = 2
nprocy    = 2          ! run with exactly 2 x 2 = 4 MPI processes
n         = 50
m         = 50
l         = 20
outname   = './comp/comp.out'
ifire     = 1          ! vegetation and fire physics on
irad      = 2          ! radiation option
u0        = 5.0        ! ambient wind (m/s) at height zu
zu        = 10
Run
mpirun -np 4 ../CODE-1.5.1/build/firerad      # from the directory holding gridlist
Not tested here The commands above follow the repository's build files and example input; I have not run FIRETEC for this page. Fuel files (treesrhof.dat, treesmoist.dat, … from Trees) and an ignition file (ignite.dat) are needed for a real burn; see TOOLS/ and the documentation as it is released.
