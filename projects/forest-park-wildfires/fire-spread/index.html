Fire-spread simulations

Before running the full WRF-SFIRE model, I built a fast fire-spread model to see what the fuel map, terrain and weather add up to. It uses the same spread equations (Rothermel's) and the same 13 fuel types as WRF-SFIRE, but holds the wind steady instead of simulating the atmosphere. That makes each fire take a fraction of a second, so I could start 2,000 random fires inside Forest Park for each of the two wind scenarios from Section 04 and let each burn for 8 hours.

Background: 60 fires from this project's spread model on the WUI fuel map. It switches with light/dark mode.

Figure 15

Burn probability

Each cell shows how many of every 1,000 fires started in the park reach it within 8 hours. Single fires are small compared with the park (the median east-wind fire covers 11 ha), so no cell burns often. The map shows where fire tends to go.

In the east wind, fires run uphill and west across the ravines and spill over the park's west edge toward the Skyline side. In the northwest wind they stay small (median 3 ha) and mostly inside the park.

Figure 16

Where a fire start matters

The reverse view: each dot is a simulated ignition, coloured by how many buildings its fire reached (fire within about 30 m of the footprint).

Fires that start deep in the park rarely get out within 8 hours: litter under the forest canopy burns slowly, about 25 m an hour. The dangerous starts are within a few hundred metres of the edge. In an east wind those are the west edge and the northwest end; in both winds, the southeast end, where the park meets the hillside streets of NW Portland.

Figure 17

One fire, two winds

The east-wind fire that reached the most buildings, re-run under both winds. The east wind stretches it about 800 m west through the forest in 8 hours (26 ha). With the northwest wind the same start burns 3 ha.

Roads and buildings stop the fire in this model, so it moves through the city only along unbroken vegetation. Real fires also jump gaps with embers, so treat the city spread here as a minimum.

Figure 18

Checking the spread model

Before trusting the maps, I checked my implementation against the spread rates Anderson (1982) published for the 13 fuel models. It matches within 10% for 11 of them. Two shrub models are further off: model 5 (−19%, 4% of the map) and model 7 (+30%, not on the map).

How it works

  • Spread rate: Rothermel's equations give each 30 m cell a head-fire speed from its fuel type, its slope, the wind and the fuel moisture of the scenario.
  • Shape: fire spreads fastest downwind and upslope and slower to the sides and back, as an ellipse that stretches with wind speed.
  • Path: arrival time at every cell is the fastest route from the ignition through the grid, in 16 directions. Roads, buildings and water can't be crossed, even diagonally.
  • Shelter: wind at flame height is reduced under tree canopy.

Limitations

  • Steady, uniform wind. One wind speed and direction everywhere, for 8 hours. In reality the terrain channels the wind and the fire makes its own. That is what WRF-SFIRE adds.
  • No embers, no house-to-house spread, no crown fire. These drive most home losses in wildland–urban fires, so building counts here are a lower bound on exposure, not a loss estimate.
  • No firefighting. Fires near the park's edges are usually reached quickly.
  • Uncalibrated city fuels. The stand-in fuels for yards and street trees from Section 02 are assumptions; the city results depend on them.
