Wildland–urban interface · Portland, Oregon · In progress

Forest Park Wildfires

Forest Park covers about 5,100 acres of forest along Portland's West Hills, with homes, industry and the Willamette River along its edges. This project models how a fire that starts in the park could spread into the neighborhoods around it, using the coupled fire–atmosphere model WRF-SFIRE and probabilistic fire-spread maps.

Section 01

Fuel maps

A fire model doesn't see trees, lawns or houses. It sees a grid, and every cell holds a fuel model: a standard description of how much fuel is there, how deep the fuel bed is and how dry it has to be to burn. Before any fire can be simulated, the park and the city need that grid.

The problem at the park's edge

The standard U.S. fuel map, LANDFIRE, marks developed land as non-burnable. That makes sense for a forest-scale study. For a city edge, it means a simulated fire stops at the park boundary every time, so the question this project asks can't be answered.

What I built

A combined map at 30 m resolution over about 19 × 20 km. Inside the park, LANDFIRE is used as published. Outside it, each developed cell gets a stand-in surface fuel based on how much of it is paved and how much tree canopy it has. Cells that are mostly buildings stay non-burnable.

Figure 3

What the urban rules changed

Only the cells that received a stand-in fuel are colored.

  • Green lines in the rural west are roads under tree canopy, which keep timber litter. Open, paved road cells are firebreaks since v2 (Figure 4).
  • A few yellow lines remain where roads are less than 30% paved, such as gravel roads.
  • Industrial land with little canopy, in Rivergate and the NW industrial district, also became grass.
  • Dark grey blocks are cells that are mostly buildings.

Figure 4 · new in v2

Fixing the roads

A 4 × 4 km window southwest of the park, where the fix changed the most cells. In v1, partly paved road cells with little canopy fell into the "open lots" rule and became a connected network of grass corridors (yellow), which would carry fire along every street. The NLCD road layer now identifies them first:

  • Freeways (US-26, I-405) are always non-burnable.
  • Other roads are non-burnable when at least 30% paved with under 40% tree canopy.
  • Roads under canopy keep their stand-in fuel, because overhanging trees and yards in the same 30 m cell still carry fire.

A first attempt made every paved street a firebreak. That turned 6,605 ha of the residential grid non-burnable and isolated every block, so I narrowed it to open roads.

Figure 5

Fuel-model families

One panel per Scott & Burgan family shows the detail behind the three map colors. Timber litter (39%), grass (31%) and timber-understory (26%) make up most of the burnable area, with grass-shrub at 4%. Shrub and slash fuels are almost absent.

Figure 6

What the rules are based on

Pavement and tree canopy from the National Land Cover Database decide each city cell's stand-in fuel. Building cover, from City of Portland footprints, decides which cells stay non-burnable.

Figure 7

Ready for WRF-SFIRE

WRF-SFIRE uses the older 13 Anderson fuel models, so the map is also exported in that system, in the binary format its preprocessing step (WPS geogrid) reads, together with the 10 m terrain (Section 02). Category 14 means "no fuel".

Limitations

  • The urban rules are a first pass. The thresholds are working assumptions and still need to be calibrated against field data and published WUI fuel studies.
  • Roads as firebreaks are an assumption. A paved road stops a surface fire, but wind-driven fires throw embers across roads, which this map can't represent.
  • Gravel and narrow roads (under 30% paved) still follow the yard rules.
  • Surface fuels only. WRF-SFIRE spreads fire through vegetation. It doesn't model houses igniting each other or embers, which cause most home losses in urban fires.

Section 02 · new in v3

Terrain for WRF-SFIRE

WRF-SFIRE needs the shape of the ground twice: once for the atmosphere, where hills steer the wind, and once for the fire, which runs faster uphill. Out of the box, the model's preprocessor uses global terrain at about 1 km, which can't see the park's ravines. I replaced it with USGS 3DEP lidar elevation for both, so the wind and the fire stand on the same ground.

Figure 8

The ground under the park

Fire runs faster uphill, so the model needs the ground shape as well as the fuel. Forest Park sits on the ridge of the Tualatin Mountains, from 12 m at the river to 339 m at the crest. Slopes inside the park have a median of 19°, and the steepest 10% exceed 29°, mostly in the ravines facing the Willamette.

This 10 m USGS 3DEP elevation becomes the fire mesh's terrain (ZSF). Rivers in 3DEP are flattened at the water surface (about 2.8 m here), so riverbanks don't get fake cliffs. Four bad cells below sea level at the domain edge were filled from their neighbors.

Figure 9

The inner grid is centered on the park, with 2.7 km between the park and its edge, and sits 1.3 km inside the fuel and terrain data. With the original data box, a grid that size would have run off the data's southern edge, past the park's south tip in NW Portland, so I extended the box.

Figure 10

The same ridge, as each grid sees it

A 9 km cross-section through the middle of the park, perpendicular to the ridge. Each grid averages the ground over its cells:

  • The 30 m fire mesh follows the 10 m lidar almost exactly, including the 195 m ravine at 4.1 km.
  • The 150 m inner atmosphere keeps the main ridge and the river-facing slope.
  • At 1,350 m the slope becomes a few large steps; the ravine disappears and the Willamette sits about 80 m up, averaged with the hillside.

That's why the fire needs its own fine terrain, and why the wind near the park comes from the 150 m grid.

What's exported for the model

  • Atmosphere terrain: 3DEP at 1 arc-second (about 30 m) over all of d01, 6,890 × 4,960 cells from sea level to Mount St. Helens (2,531 m). It agrees with the 10 m data to within 0.06 m on average.
  • Fire terrain: 3DEP at 10 m over the fuel-map area.
  • Setup files: a domain configuration and the lookup-table entries that point the model at these files.

Limitations

  • Not yet run. The grids are a proposal. They still need to pass through the model's preprocessor and a test simulation, which may call for terrain smoothing on the 150 m grid.
  • Approximate averaging. The resolution comparison uses simple block averages; the preprocessor's own averaging will differ slightly.
