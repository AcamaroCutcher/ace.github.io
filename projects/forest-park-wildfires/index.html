Wildland–urban interface · Portland, Oregon · In progress

Forest Park Wildfires

Forest Park covers about 5,100 acres of forest along Portland's West Hills, with homes, industry and the Willamette River along its edges. This project models how a fire that starts in the park could spread into the neighborhoods around it, using the coupled fire–atmosphere model WRF-SFIRE and probabilistic fire-spread maps.

Section 01

Fuel maps

A fire model doesn't see trees, lawns or houses. It sees a grid, and every cell holds a fuel model: a standard description of how much fuel is there, how deep the fuel bed is and how dry it has to be to burn. Before any fire can be simulated, the park and the city need that grid.

The problem at the park's edge

The standard U.S. fuel map, LANDFIRE, marks developed land as non-burnable. That makes sense for a forest-scale study. For a city edge, it means a simulated fire stops at the park boundary every time, so the question this project asks can't be answered.

What I built

A combined map at 30 m resolution over about 19 × 20 km. Inside the park, LANDFIRE is used as published. Outside it, each developed cell gets a stand-in surface fuel based on how much of it is paved and how much tree canopy it has. Cells that are mostly buildings stay non-burnable.

What the urban rules changed

  • Industrial land with little canopy, in Rivergate and the NW industrial district, also became grass.
  • Dark grey blocks are cells that are mostly buildings.

Figure 4 · new in v2

Fixing the roads

A 4 × 4 km window southwest of the park, where the fix changed the most cells. In v1, partly paved road cells with little canopy fell into the "open lots" rule and became a connected network of grass corridors (yellow), which would carry fire along every street. The NLCD road layer now identifies them first:

  • Freeways (US-26, I-405) are always non-burnable.
  • Other roads are non-burnable when at least 30% paved with under 40% tree canopy.
  • Roads under canopy keep their stand-in fuel, because overhanging trees and yards in the same 30 m cell still carry fire.

A first attempt made every paved street a firebreak. That turned 6,605 ha of the residential grid non-burnable and isolated every block, so I narrowed it to open roads.

Figure 5

Fuel-model families

One panel per Scott & Burgan family shows the detail behind the three map colors. Timber litter (39%), grass (31%) and timber-understory (26%) make up most of the burnable area, with grass-shrub at 4%. Shrub and slash fuels are almost absent.

Figure 6

What the rules are based on

Pavement and tree canopy from the National Land Cover Database decide each city cell's stand-in fuel. Building cover, from City of Portland footprints, decides which cells stay non-burnable.

Figure 7

WRF-SFIRE uses the older 13 Anderson fuel models, so the map is also exported in that system, in the binary format its preprocessing step (WPS geogrid) reads, together with the 10 m terrain (Section 02). Category 14 means "no fuel".

Limitations

  • The urban rules are a first pass. The thresholds are working assumptions and still need to be calibrated against field data and published WUI fuel studies.
  • Roads as firebreaks are an assumption. A paved road stops a surface fire, but wind-driven fires throw embers across roads, which this map can't represent.
  • Gravel and narrow roads (under 30% paved) still follow the yard rules.
  • Surface fuels only. WRF-SFIRE spreads fire through vegetation. It doesn't model houses igniting each other or embers, which cause most home losses in urban fires.

Section 02 · v3

Terrain for WRF-SFIRE

WRF-SFIRE needs the shape of the ground twice: once for the atmosphere, where hills steer the wind, and once for the fire, which runs faster uphill. Out of the box, the model's preprocessor uses global terrain at about 1 km, which can't see the park's ravines. I replaced it with USGS 3DEP lidar elevation for both, so the wind and the fire stand on the same ground.

Figure 8

The ground under the park

Fire runs faster uphill, so the model needs the ground shape as well as the fuel. Forest Park sits on the ridge of the Tualatin Mountains, from 12 m at the river to 339 m at the crest. Slopes inside the park have a median of 19°, and the steepest 10% exceed 29°, mostly in the ravines facing the Willamette.

This 10 m USGS 3DEP elevation becomes the fire mesh's terrain (ZSF). Rivers in 3DEP are flattened at the water surface (about 2.8 m here), so riverbanks don't get fake cliffs. Four bad cells below sea level at the domain edge were filled from their neighbors.

Figure 9

Three nested model grids

Weather models zoom in through nested grids, each three times finer than the one around it. The outermost takes weather from a forecast model at 3 km; the innermost runs the fire.

Figure 10

The same ridge, as each grid sees it

A 9 km cross-section through the middle of the park, perpendicular to the ridge. Each grid averages the ground over its cells:

  • The 30 m fire mesh follows the 10 m lidar almost exactly, including the 195 m ravine at 4.1 km.
  • The 150 m inner atmosphere keeps the main ridge and the river-facing slope.
  • At 1,350 m the slope becomes a few large steps; the ravine disappears and the Willamette sits about 80 m up, averaged with the hillside.

That's why the fire needs its own fine terrain, and why the wind near the park comes from the 150 m grid.

What's exported for the model

  • Atmosphere terrain: 3DEP at 1 arc-second (about 30 m) over all of d01, 6,890 × 4,960 cells from sea level to Mount St. Helens (2,531 m). It agrees with the 10 m data to within 0.06 m on average.
  • Fire terrain: 3DEP at 10 m over the fuel-map area.
  • Setup files: a domain configuration and the lookup-table entries that point the model at these files.

Limitations

  • Not yet run. The grids are a proposal. They still need to pass through the model's preprocessor and a test simulation, which may call for terrain smoothing on the 150 m grid.
  • Approximate averaging. The resolution comparison uses simple block averages; the preprocessor's own averaging will differ slightly.

Section 03 · v4

Fire weather

A fuel map and terrain say where fire can go; the weather decides where it does. To choose realistic wind and dryness for the simulations, and real dates to run WRF-SFIRE on, I analysed hourly observations from June to October, 2000–2025, at the four airports around the park: Portland International (13 km east of the park's centre), Hillsboro (15 km west), Scappoose (23 km north) and Troutdale (28 km east, at the mouth of the Columbia River Gorge).

Figure 11

Dangerous winds come from the east

I counted an hour as dry and windy when relative humidity was 25% or lower and the wind reached 15 mph, or gusts 25 mph. That's my screening threshold, not the National Weather Service's official Red Flag criteria.

On an ordinary summer hour the wind at Portland International blows from the northwest. On dry and windy hours, 92% blow from the northeast to southeast. This is the east wind that pours out of the Columbia River Gorge when high pressure builds east of the Cascades, bringing dry inland air. Hillsboro, on the far side of the park, sees the same thing (83%). Scappoose is the exception, but it logged only 46 such hours in 26 years.

Figure 12

Late summer, not midsummer

Portland International averages about 17 dry and windy hours a year, and 76% fall in September or October. July is hot but rarely windy enough. The danger window is the end of the dry season, when fuels have had all summer to dry and the first east-wind events of autumn arrive before the rains.

Figure 13

The case study: Labor Day 2020

The same east wind drove the 2020 Labor Day fires in the Oregon Cascades. At Portland International it lasted from the afternoon of 7 September to the evening of the 9th: 44 dry and windy hours, sustained winds to 36 mph, gusts to 50 mph and humidity down to 8%.

The bottom panel estimates the moisture of fine dead fuel (twigs, grass, needles) from temperature and humidity. It fell from 16% to about 4% within a day and reached 2% on the 9th. Fuels that dry burn readily.

Figure 14

Where wind and slope work together

Fire spreads fastest when wind and slope push the same way. Coloured cells are slopes of 10° or more that face into the wind, so the wind drives fire straight uphill there.

An east wind lines up with 42% of the park's slopes: the river-facing hillside, from Linnton and Highway 30 up to the ridge. Over the ridge, a fire would be heading for the homes along Skyline Boulevard and the Washington County side. The usual northwest wind lines up with 28% and pushes fire along the park's length, toward its southeast end in NW Portland.

Limitations

  • Airports, not the park. The stations are 13–28 km away on open, flat ground. Inside the park, terrain channels the wind and the forest floor stays moister than air-based estimates suggest. WRF-SFIRE will resolve the local wind; the fuel moisture still needs care.
  • Dead fuel only. The moisture estimate covers fine dead fuel from that hour's temperature and humidity. It ignores rain in the days before (common before October events) and the moisture of living plants.
  • Hours, not fires. A dry, windy hour is an opportunity, not a prediction. Ignition is a separate question.

Section 04 · new in v5 · prototype

Fire-spread simulations

Before running the full WRF-SFIRE model, I built a fast fire-spread model to see what the fuel map, terrain and weather add up to. It uses the same spread equations (Rothermel's) and the same 13 fuel types as WRF-SFIRE, but holds the wind steady instead of simulating the atmosphere. That makes each fire take a fraction of a second, so I could start 2,000 random fires inside Forest Park for each of the two wind scenarios from Section 03 and let each burn for 8 hours.

Figure 15

Burn probability

Each cell shows how many of every 1,000 fires started in the park reach it within 8 hours. Single fires are small compared with the park (the median east-wind fire covers 11 ha), so no cell burns often. The map shows where fire tends to go.

In the east wind, fires run uphill and west across the ravines and spill over the park's west edge toward the Skyline side. In the northwest wind they stay small (median 3 ha) and mostly inside the park.

Figure 16

Where a fire start matters

The reverse view: each dot is a simulated ignition, coloured by how many buildings its fire reached (fire within about 30 m of the footprint).

Fires that start deep in the park rarely get out within 8 hours: litter under the forest canopy burns slowly, about 25 m an hour. The dangerous starts are within a few hundred metres of the edge. In an east wind those are the west edge and the northwest end; in both winds, the southeast end, where the park meets the hillside streets of NW Portland.

Figure 17

One fire, two winds

The east-wind fire that reached the most buildings, re-run under both winds. The east wind stretches it about 800 m west through the forest in 8 hours (26 ha). With the northwest wind the same start burns 3 ha.

Roads and buildings stop the fire in this model, so it moves through the city only along unbroken vegetation. Real fires also jump gaps with embers, so treat the city spread here as a minimum.

Figure 18

Checking the spread model

Before trusting the maps, I checked my implementation against the spread rates Anderson (1982) published for the 13 fuel models. It matches within 10% for 11 of them. Two shrub models are further off: model 5 (−19%, 4% of the map) and model 7 (+30%, not on the map).

How it works

  • Spread rate: Rothermel's equations give each 30 m cell a head-fire speed from its fuel type, its slope, the wind and the fuel moisture of the scenario.
  • Shape: fire spreads fastest downwind and upslope and slower to the sides and back, as an ellipse that stretches with wind speed.
  • Path: arrival time at every cell is the fastest route from the ignition through the grid, in 16 directions. Roads, buildings and water can't be crossed, even diagonally.
  • Shelter: wind at flame height is reduced under tree canopy.

Limitations

  • Steady, uniform wind. One wind speed and direction everywhere, for 8 hours. In reality the terrain channels the wind and the fire makes its own. That is what WRF-SFIRE adds.
  • No embers, no house-to-house spread, no crown fire. These drive most home losses in wildland–urban fires, so building counts here are a lower bound on exposure, not a loss estimate.
  • No firefighting. Fires near the park's edges are usually reached quickly.
  • Uncalibrated city fuels. The stand-in fuels for yards and street trees from Section 01 are assumptions; the city results depend on them.
