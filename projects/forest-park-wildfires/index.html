Wildland–urban interface · Portland, Oregon · In progress

Forest Park Wildfires

Forest Park covers about 5,100 acres of forest along Portland's West Hills, with homes, industry and the Willamette River along its edges. This project models how a fire that starts in the park could spread into the neighborhoods around it, using the coupled fire–atmosphere model WRF-SFIRE and probabilistic fire-spread maps.

Section 01

Fuel maps

A fire model doesn't see trees, lawns or houses. It sees a grid, and every cell holds a fuel model: a standard description of how much fuel is there, how deep the fuel bed is and how dry it has to be to burn. Before any fire can be simulated, the park and the city need that grid.

The problem at the park's edge

The standard U.S. fuel map, LANDFIRE, marks developed land as non-burnable. That makes sense for a forest-scale study. For a city edge, it means a simulated fire stops at the park boundary every time, so the question this project asks can't be answered.

What I built

A combined map at 30 m resolution. Inside the park, LANDFIRE is used as published. Outside it, each developed cell gets a stand-in surface fuel based on how much of it is paved and how much tree canopy it has. Cells that are mostly buildings stay non-burnable.

Figure 1

The combined fuel map

Three colorblind-safe colors carry the burnable fuel groups. Greys are non-burnable, pale blue is water, and the black outline is Forest Park.

  • The park is mostly timber-understory (violet) and timber litter (green). The heaviest model, TU5 (very high load, dry timber-shrub), covers about 846 ha inside the park.
  • Treed neighborhoods south and west of the park, in the NW Hills and Forest Heights, form continuous timber litter joined to the park.
  • Open, lightly paved areas such as St. Johns become grass (yellow).

Figure 2

Before and after

On the left, fuel ends at the park boundary. On the right, the city's vegetated cells carry fuel, so a model can move fire from the park edge into yards and street trees.

Figure 3

What the urban rules changed

Only the cells that received a stand-in fuel are colored.

  • The thin yellow lines across the rural west are road cells that became grass. Roads should act more like firebreaks, so this is the next thing to fix.
  • Industrial land with little canopy, in Rivergate and the NW industrial district, also became grass.
  • Dark grey blocks are cells that are mostly buildings.

Figure 4

Fuel-model families

One panel per Scott & Burgan family shows the detail behind the three map colors. Grass (36%), timber litter (34%) and timber-understory (25%) make up nearly all the burnable area. Shrub and slash fuels are almost absent.

Figure 5

What the rules are based on

Pavement and tree canopy from the National Land Cover Database decide each city cell's stand-in fuel. Building cover, from City of Portland footprints, decides which cells stay non-burnable.

Figure 6

Ready for WRF-SFIRE

WRF-SFIRE uses the older 13 Anderson fuel models, so the map is also exported in that system, in the binary format its preprocessing step (WPS geogrid) reads. Category 14 means "no fuel".

Limitations

  • The urban rules are a first pass. The thresholds are working assumptions and still need to be calibrated against field data and published WUI fuel studies.
  • Roads become grass. Road cells will be separated out and treated as firebreaks.
  • Surface fuels only. WRF-SFIRE spreads fire through vegetation. It doesn't model houses igniting each other or embers, which cause most home losses in urban fires.
  • No terrain yet. High-resolution elevation for the fire grid is the next layer to add.
