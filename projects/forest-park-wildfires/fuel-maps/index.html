Section 02

Fuel maps

A fire model doesn't see trees, lawns or houses. It sees a grid, and every cell holds a fuel model: a standard description of how much fuel is there, how deep the fuel bed is and how dry it has to be to burn. Before any fire can be simulated, the park and the city need that grid.

Background: 60 fires from this project's spread model on the WUI fuel map. It switches with light/dark mode.

The problem at the park's edge

The standard U.S. fuel map, LANDFIRE, marks developed land as non-burnable. That makes sense for a forest-scale study. For a city edge, it means a simulated fire stops at the park boundary every time, so the question this project asks can't be answered.

What I built

A combined map at 30 m resolution over about 19 × 20 km. Inside the park, LANDFIRE is used as published. Outside it, each developed cell gets a stand-in surface fuel based on how much of it is paved and how much tree canopy it has. Cells that are mostly buildings stay non-burnable.

What the urban rules changed

Only the cells that received a stand-in fuel are colored.

  • Green lines in the rural west are roads under tree canopy, which keep timber litter. Open, paved road cells are firebreaks since v2 (Figure 4).
  • A few yellow lines remain where roads are less than 30% paved, such as gravel roads.
  • In v2, industrial land with little canopy, in Rivergate and the NW industrial district, also became grass. Version 3 removes that fuel (Figure 8).
  • Dark grey blocks are cells that are mostly buildings.

Figure 4 · new in v2

Fixing the roads

A 4 × 4 km window southwest of the park, where the fix changed the most cells. In v1, partly paved road cells with little canopy fell into the "open lots" rule and became a connected network of grass corridors (yellow), which would carry fire along every street. The NLCD road layer now identifies them first:

  • Freeways (US-26, I-405) are always non-burnable.
  • Other roads are non-burnable when at least 30% paved with under 40% tree canopy.
  • Roads under canopy keep their stand-in fuel, because overhanging trees and yards in the same 30 m cell still carry fire.

A first attempt made every paved street a firebreak. That turned 6,605 ha of the residential grid non-burnable and isolated every block, so I narrowed it to open roads.

Fuel-model families

One panel per Scott & Burgan family shows the detail behind the three map colors. Timber litter (40%), grass (30%) and timber-understory (26%) make up most of the burnable area, with grass-shrub at 4%. Shrub and slash fuels are almost absent.

Figure 6

What the rules are based on

Pavement and tree canopy from the National Land Cover Database decide each city cell's stand-in fuel. Building cover, from City of Portland footprints, decides which cells stay non-burnable.

Figure 7

Ready for WRF-SFIRE

WRF-SFIRE uses the older 13 Anderson fuel models, so the map is also exported in that system, in the binary format its preprocessing step (WPS geogrid) reads, together with the 10 m terrain (Section 03). Category 14 means "no fuel".

New in version 3: what the land is used for

The v2 rules know how much of a cell is paved and how much is under trees, but not what the rest of the cell is. In a rail yard or a container terminal the rest is gravel and steel, and v2 called it grass. Version 3 adds land use from OpenStreetMap and the NLCD land-cover class to correct four things, all outside the park.

The four corrections

  • Industrial, commercial and parking land with under 40% canopy loses its stand-in fuel.
  • Irrigated turf (golf courses, sports fields, cemeteries) is capped at the sparsest grass model.
  • Pasture and hay, which LANDFIRE treats as non-burnable farmland, gets short grass.
  • Islands of one or two stand-in cells that no surface fire can reach are removed.
  • 284 haIndustrial and commercial land no longer burnable
  • 215 haUnreachable fuel islands removed
  • 239 haPasture given a grass fuel
  • 106 haIrrigated turf set to sparse grass

What version 3 changed

Only the changed cells are colored.

  • Orange: stand-in grass removed from the industrial waterfront (Rivergate, the NW industrial district, Swan Island) and from business parks in the Tualatin Valley.
  • Yellow: pasture in the west hills and on Sauvie Island, now short grass.
  • Green: golf courses and playing fields, now the sparsest grass.
  • Dark grey: single cells of yard fuel scattered through the street grid.
  • Nothing inside the park boundary changed: LANDFIRE is still used there as published.

Figure 9 · new in v3

The canopy above the surface fuels

A surface fuel map says nothing about the trees overhead. Version 3 adds LANDFIRE's four canopy layers on the same 30 m grid: height, cover, base height and bulk density. Inside the park the canopy averages 25 m tall with 64% cover, and its base sits a median 2 m above the ground, low enough for a hot surface fire to reach. WRF-SFIRE's default fire model does not use these layers. The 3-D simulator uses them to draw the forest and for its optional crown-fire test.

Did it change the answer?

I re-ran the 4,000 fires of Section 05 on the new map. The results moved by a tenth of a percentage point: the corrections are far from the park, on land a park fire rarely reaches in eight hours. The fire-spread results therefore stand, and they no longer depend on grass that was never there.

Limitations

  • The urban rules are a first pass. The thresholds are working assumptions and still need to be calibrated against field data and published WUI fuel studies.
  • Roads as firebreaks are an assumption. A paved road stops a surface fire, but wind-driven fires throw embers across roads, which this map can't represent.
  • Gravel and narrow roads (under 30% paved) still follow the yard rules.
  • Land use is volunteered data. OpenStreetMap polygons are drawn by hand and are uneven: a yard that nobody has mapped as industrial keeps its v2 fuel.
  • Pasture is assumed cured. Giving hay and pasture a grass fuel fits September and October. Irrigated or freshly cut fields would not burn.
  • Surface fuels only. WRF-SFIRE spreads fire through vegetation. It doesn't model houses igniting each other or embers, which cause most home losses in urban fires.
