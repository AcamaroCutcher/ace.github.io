Inside the subduction zone

The Juan de Fuca Plate dives east beneath North America. Here is that plate in 3D, from the USGS Slab2 model, with every recorded M4+ earthquake since 1980 placed at its true depth. Each one is colored by where it happened: in the crust above, inside the sinking plate, or on the boundary between them. These are the report's three kinds of earthquakes.

How each earthquake is classified

Each quake's depth is compared with the depth of the plate surface directly beneath it (Slab2, interpolated):

  • Inside the sinking plate (intraslab): at or below 5 km above the plate's top surface, where the plate has already sunk at least 20 km. The 5 km allows for earthquake-depth uncertainty. Quakes inside the still-shallow plate near the trench count as offshore instead.
  • Near the plate boundary (megathrust): 5–12 km above the plate top, where the plate is shallower than 35 km.
  • In the crust above (North American Plate): shallower than that.
  • Offshore plates and faults: west of the trench, where Slab2 has no subducting plate, or inside the plate before it has sunk 20 km. This includes the busy Gorda Plate and Mendocino fault zone off northern California, such as the 1980 M7.2 Eureka earthquake.

This is a simple geometric rule for illustration. Depths of offshore quakes are poorly constrained, and some "near the boundary" quakes near Cape Mendocino may belong to other faults in that complex area.
