Cascadia: what are the odds?

A scrolling walkthrough of the USGS report Earthquake Probabilities and Hazards in the U.S. Pacific Northwest (Fact Sheet 2025–3050): three kinds of earthquakes, how often each one strikes, and what that means for the next 50 years.

Scroll to begin ↓

Two plates collide

Off the Pacific Northwest coast, the Juan de Fuca Plate slides east beneath the North American Plate at a few centimeters per year. This is the Cascadia Subduction Zone, running from northern California to Vancouver Island.

The graphic is a real slice through the ground at the latitude of Seattle. The sinking plate's shape comes from the USGS Slab2 model, and the dots are earthquakes recorded since 1980. Depth is stretched ×2.5.

1. Shallow crustal quakes

The North American Plate gets squeezed, and faults in its crust break: the Seattle, Tacoma and southern Whidbey Island faults, among others. The Seattle Fault produced an M7.5–7.8 around 923–924 C.E.

Chance of an M6.5+ in the Puget Sound region in the next 50 years: 17%.

2. Deep (intraslab) quakes

Inside the sinking plate, 40–60 km down, the rock bends and releases fluids, and it breaks. These quakes are the region's most common damaging type: 1949 Olympia (M6.8), 1965 Seattle (M6.5), 2001 Nisqually (M6.8).

Chance of an M6.5+ under Puget Sound in the next 50 years: 85%.

3. The megathrust: the "Big One"

The boundary between the plates is locked. It stores strain for centuries and then slips all at once, lifting the sea floor, dropping the coast and launching a tsunami. The last time was January 26, 1700, an estimated M8.7–9.2.

Chance of an M~9 in the next 50 years: 10–15%.

How often does each one strike?

On average: a deep M6.5+ under Puget Sound about every 26 years, a crustal M6.5+ about every 265 years, and an M9 on the whole coast about every 500 years.

The graphic shows one possible next 300 years, drawn at random at those rates. The timing really is irregular.

Turning "how often" into "how likely"

If quakes have no memory, the chance of at least one in t years is P = 1 − e−t/τ, where τ is the average time between them. The report uses t = 50 years, about the life of a typical building.

The next 50 years

The most likely damaging quake isn't the Big One. It's a deep quake under Puget Sound. Crustal faults and the megathrust are less likely, but they can be far more destructive.

Does the fault remember 1700?

If strain builds steadily, a quake gets more likely the longer it's been. 326 years after 1700, a "memory" model raises the M9 odds from 10% to about 15%, which is why the report gives a range.

It has happened many times

Sediment layers shaken loose by great quakes record at least 19 whole-coast ruptures in ~10,000 years, plus more partial ones in the south. That's why the M9 is a matter of when, not if.
