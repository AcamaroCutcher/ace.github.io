10,000 years of great Cascadia earthquakes

Nobody has recorded a Cascadia megathrust earthquake with instruments, yet it is "the most paleoseismically well-constrained subduction zone in the world" (p. 3). Each great quake shook loose underwater landslides that left layers of sediment (turbidites) on the sea floor. Dating those layers in cores from Vancouver Island to northern California reveals the quakes below.

How we know

Turbidites

Strong shaking sends sediment avalanches down submarine canyons. When the same layers show up in canyons hundreds of kilometers apart at the same time, a single great quake is the likely cause. These are the bars above.

Ghost forests

In 1700 the coast dropped and seawater killed whole forests. Tree rings show the trees at Copalis, Washington, died in the winter of 1699–1700.

Tsunami sand

Sheets of ocean sand buried in coastal marshes and lakes record tsunamis, often right above suddenly sunken soils.

Written and oral records

A tsunami with no local earthquake reached Japan on January 27–28, 1700: the "orphan tsunami". Indigenous oral histories also describe great earthquakes. Project 10 will tell this story.

It has happened again and again

Great earthquakes have struck Cascadia dozens of times in 10,000 years. The next one is a question of when, not if.

The gaps are irregular

Gaps between whole-coast ruptures ranged from about two centuries to over a thousand years. That's why the report gives probabilities, not dates.

The south shakes more often

Partial ruptures add to the whole-coast ones in southern Cascadia, so it averages one great quake every ~300–350 years, not ~500.
