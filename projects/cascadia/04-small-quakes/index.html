Small quakes predict big ones

Large crustal earthquakes under Puget Sound are too rare to count directly, but small ones are not. The report counts 20 small crustal quakes (M4.0–5.4) from 1960 to 2024, then uses the Gutenberg–Richter rule to estimate an M6.5+ crustal quake about every 280 years (p. 5). That's close to the ~265 years found from geological evidence. Here's how it works.

The rule: every step up in magnitude is several times rarer

Around the world, earthquake counts follow the Gutenberg–Richter law: log10N = a − b·M. N is the number of quakes of magnitude M or larger, and b, the "b-value", sets how fast big quakes get rarer. The report uses b = 0.8, typical of the western United States.

Small quakes are a census

We can't wait centuries to count M6.5s, but a few decades of M4s are enough to estimate their rate, if big and small quakes follow the same rule.

Two independent methods agree

Small-quake counting (~280–320 years) and geological evidence of past ruptures (~265 years) give similar answers. That agreement is why the report trusts the 17% chance in 50 years.

The b-value is the weak spot

Twenty quakes can't pin down b precisely. The report borrows the western-U.S. value (0.8). With b = 1.0, the same 20 quakes would imply an M6.5+ only about every 1,000 years.
